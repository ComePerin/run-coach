# The Norwegian Method for Amateur Runners — Coaching Reference

> **Purpose of this document:** a knowledge base for an AI running coach. It describes an amateur-adapted version of the Norwegian training method (sub-threshold, "double-threshold"-inspired training) so the coach can build a viable, repeatable training plan for 3 to 7 running sessions per week.
>
> **Source:** YouTube video *"Norwegian method for amateurs — 5 levels from 3 to 7 sessions/week"* (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UkyBDISZ-Gc), French running creator, transcript-based.
>
> **Legend:** Content without a tag comes directly from the video. Items tagged **[Coach heuristic]** are reasonable defaults added to fill gaps the video leaves open. The coach may adjust them.

---

## 1. Why this method suits amateurs

- The core idea: **do as much work as possible, given what the athlete can absorb and recover from week after week, without breaking down.**
- Elites run 12–14 sessions/week and have optimal recovery (9 h sleep, naps, nutrition, physio on demand). Amateurs have jobs, families, social lives and limited, often disrupted sleep.
- **Recovery capacity is the amateur's most limited resource.** A method designed to maximize work *under a recovery constraint* is therefore particularly well-suited.
- The method is **cross-distance**. A runner doing fewer than ~7 runs/week has most likely not exhausted their aerobic potential. Until they do, developing aerobic capacity is the most profitable lever **whatever the target distance** (5K, half marathon, marathon, trail, local race). Distance-specific and profile-specific differences only become a real issue near one's aerobic ceiling.

## 2. The three founding principles

### 2.1 Repeatable structure
- Define **one typical week** and **repeat it** for many weeks.
- If every week is different, every week is a gamble: too little to progress, or too much and you get injured, with no way of knowing which happened.
- A repeated week has a **known cost and a known benefit**.
- To adjust, **move only one variable ("slider") at a time.**

### 2.2 Intensity control
- Most quality work is done **just below the second threshold** (LT2 / anaerobic threshold).
- **Above LT2**, fatigue and injury risk rise disproportionately.
- **Below LT2**, much more volume can be accumulated.
- The sub-LT2 zone has the **best ratio between intensity and tolerable volume**. Slower requires far more time. Faster means you can't accumulate enough and you pay for it in recovery.

### 2.3 Long general preparation
- Almost everything is built during **general preparation**.
- **Specific preparation is short.** It converts what has been built into race pace and gives confidence.
- **The plans in this document are general-preparation plans.** That is where the Norwegian method truly differs. Race-specific preparation (session X sliding toward race pace, see §6.4) is a separate phase.

## 3. Intensity zones and how to pace them

### 3.1 Easy runs (jogs) and long runs
- Run at a **really easy pace**: generally **≤ 70–75% of max heart rate**.
- **The real criterion is talk-test: being able to hold a conversation in full sentences.** For beginners whose HR rises quickly when jogging, do **not** force walk breaks just to stay under the HR number; the conversation criterion takes priority.
- Goal: stimulate without mechanical overload, and **keep freshness for quality days.**
- **The long run is at the same pace as the jogs, just longer.** No progression, no steady-pace segments, no marathon-pace blocks.

### 3.2 "Threshold" means a physiological state, not a pace
- A threshold session means staying in the state **between the first (LT1) and second (LT2) thresholds**, from the first rep to the last.
- **Key consequence (the most common mistake):** the pace changes from one session to another.
  - The longer the repetition, the more time the heart rate has to drift upward, so **the slower you must start.**
  - 45-second reps and 10-minute reps are not run at the same speed, yet both are threshold sessions physiologically.
- **Hard rule: never exceed LT2.** If in doubt, go slower.

### 3.3 Finding threshold pace — two options

**Option 1 — Heart-rate thresholds (preferred if HR thresholds are known)**
1. Start at a pace that puts HR **just above LT1 heart rate**.
2. Let the cardiac drift do the work: HR slowly rises toward LT2.
3. **Only rule: if HR reaches LT2 heart rate, slow down.**

**Option 2 — From recent race times**
1. Derive a theoretical threshold pace from a recent race (using a race-equivalence / threshold calculator).
2. **Slow down according to repetition length.**
3. Worked example from the video:
   - Half marathon in 1:30:00 → estimated threshold pace ≈ **4:11 /km**.
   - Session: 3 × 10 min (long reps) → slow down by **6–9%** → **4:26–4:34 /km**.
   - The reps are run at **~4:30 /km, not 4:11 /km.**

**[Coach heuristic]** The video only gives the 6–9% slowdown for 10-min reps. Suggested starting adjustments for other formats (to be validated with HR/RPE and refined over the weeks):

| Rep length | Slowdown vs. theoretical threshold pace |
|---|---|
| ~45 s – 1 min | ~0–2% (close to threshold pace) |
| ~3 min | ~2–4% |
| ~5–6 min | ~4–6% |
| ~10 min | **6–9% (from video)** |

Whatever the table says, **HR must stay below LT2** and RPE should stay "controlled" (never a race effort).

## 4. Threshold sessions

### 4.1 Standard warm-up (identical for every threshold session)
Its sole purpose is to raise the pace gradually without abrupt changes.

1. **10 min easy jog**
2. **5 min steady**: around marathon pace, effort ~**5/10**
3. **5 min at LT1**: a bit faster, very controlled, effort ~**6/10**
4. → Start the main set.

- If unsure of steady/LT1 paces, don't worry: the goal is just to step from jog pace to threshold pace via two intermediate steps, so the body isn't shocked.
- If the athlete already has a warm-up that works, they can keep it.
- This warm-up puts **20 min in the legs** before the main set, **10 of which are "active"** (steady + LT1). These count toward the session's quality.

### 4.2 Session formats
The video recommends **four formats, from 45-second to 10-minute repetitions.** Same logic for all: **the longer the rep, the longer the recovery and the slower the pace.**

Formats explicitly mentioned in the video:
- **3 × 10 min** (or 2 × 10 min)
- **5 × 6 min**
- **4 × 5 min**
- **10 × 3 min**
- A **45-second rep** format (exact sets/recoveries shown on screen only, not in the transcript)

**[Coach heuristic]** Suggested recoveries and a 45-s format, consistent with standard Norwegian-style practice:

| Format | Typical recovery | Time at threshold |
|---|---|---|
| 20–25 × 45 s | 15 s easy jog / standing | 15–19 min |
| 10 × 3 min | 30–60 s | 30 min |
| 4–5 × 5–6 min | ~1 min | 20–30 min |
| 2–3 × 10 min | 1.5–2 min | 20–30 min |

### 4.3 Which format to choose
- **All formats are effective.** Progress comes from the **sequence of weeks**, not from the choice of session per se.
- Choose the format the athlete **enjoys most**, or **alternate week to week.**
- If the athlete has no preference → **choose the longest reps (10 min)**. They help most for races of 10 km and beyond.
- With 2+ threshold sessions per week, **use different formats** to cover a wider range of paces and to stay mentally fresh over ~3 months of repetition.

## 5. Prerequisite (before level 1)

- **3 sessions/week is the minimum for serious progress.** That's when the runner runs often enough to progress and when threshold work starts having an impact.
- If the athlete **cannot yet run 3 × 45–60 min per week**, **don't do intervals.** Just progressively build easy running until reaching 3 runs/week of 45–60 min. Structured sessions won't help much before that.

## 6. The five levels (typical weeks)

> General rule: day placement is up to the athlete's life constraints, **as long as the stated organizational rules are respected.** The suggested weekly layouts are examples, marked **[Coach heuristic]** unless the video specified the day.

### 6.1 Level 1 — 3 sessions/week
| Session | Content |
|---|---|
| Easy jog | 45–60 min |
| Threshold | 45–60 min total incl. warm-up, **~20 min at threshold** (+ the 10 active warm-up minutes) |
| Long run | 60–75 min, easy |

- **Rule: at least one rest day between each session.**
- Any format; with no preference → 2 × 10 min.
- **[Coach heuristic]** Example: Tue threshold · Thu jog · Sat or Sun long run.

### 6.2 Level 2 — 4 sessions/week
- **2 of 4 sessions are threshold sessions.** This is where the athlete really enters the Norwegian philosophy.
- Quality is **~20–25% of total volume**, at controlled paces → sustainable week after week, **provided the athlete stays below LT2.**
- Use **two different formats**, e.g. **2 × 10 min early in the week** and **4 × 5 min for the second.**
- **[Coach heuristic]** Example: Tue threshold (2 × 10') · Thu jog · Fri or Sat threshold (4 × 5') · Sun long run. Avoid two threshold sessions on consecutive days at this level.

### 6.3 Level 3 — 5 sessions/week
Two changes vs. level 2:
1. **Add one easy jog.**
2. **The second threshold session is placed the day right before the long run.** This sequence is used again at the next levels, so the athlete gets used to it now. It works because the long run isn't very long and is **really easy**.
- Threshold time per session goes from **~20 to ~30 min.** Solid but manageable sessions, as long as the athlete **never crosses LT2** and the long run stays really easy.
- **[Coach heuristic]** Example: Mon rest · Tue threshold · Wed jog · Thu rest · Fri jog · Sat threshold · Sun long run.

### 6.4 Level 4 — 6 sessions/week (3 quality days, two variants)
This is the most debated choice of the method.

**Variant A — Three threshold sessions** (the creator's "simplified Norwegian method")
- Vary the three: e.g. **3 × 10 min, 5 × 6 min, 10 × 3 min.**
- Covers a wide pace range, **from ~marathon pace to ~15 km pace, always below LT2.**
- Very effective, but divisive. Many runners can't trust a plan that never goes above threshold.

**Variant B — Two threshold sessions + "Session X"** (closer to the classic elite Norwegian method)
- Two thresholds already deliver the essentials. Adding a bit of higher intensity can be worth it.
- **Preferred session X: hill repeats.** They develop strength and power with **less mechanical impact than flat running** → lower injury risk → best benefit/risk ratio for adding intensity.
- **Session X is adjustable:**
  - At the first sign of overload or pain → reduce it or **replace it with an easy jog**. The week stays on track.
  - If all goes well → experiment with **5 km / 10 km pace or vVO2max (VMA)** work.
  - As a race approaches, this slot **gradually slides toward race-specific pace** (specific preparation phase).
- **Mandatory rule: 2 easy days after session X.** In the video's plan: **session X on Saturday → easy long run on Sunday → rest on Monday.**
  - Why: a session above threshold costs more, but above all its cost is **unpredictable**. Based on Marius Bakken's muscle-tone experiments when developing the classic Norwegian method, **a single easy day cannot be guaranteed to absorb a supra-threshold session.**

**[Coach heuristic]** Example layouts:
- *Variant A:* Mon rest · Tue threshold (3 × 10') · Wed jog · Thu threshold (10 × 3') · Fri jog · Sat threshold (5 × 6') · Sun long run.
- *Variant B:* Mon rest · Tue threshold · Wed jog · Thu threshold · Fri jog · **Sat session X (hills)** · Sun long run (easy).

### 6.5 Level 5 — 7 sessions/week
- **Same as level 4 + an easy jog on Monday.** Both variants A and B remain identical.
- This 7th day matters: no buffer day, no full rest anymore, and a real **mental cost of never stopping.**
- **The Monday jog is the primary adjustment variable.** It can be removed any week, without breaking anything and **without guilt**. Its gain is real but **marginal** compared with the rest.

## 7. When to move up a level

Evaluate **three signals together over the last 2–3 weeks.** **All three must be "yes"; one "no" → stay at the current level.**

1. **No settling pain.** Pain lasting **> 2 days**, **recurring in the same place**, or **altering the stride** = "no".
2. **Fatigue dissipates.** The athlete arrives fresh for quality sessions; the jog the day after a threshold session feels easy, not a chore. Carrying baseline fatigue from one week to the next = current load is already enough.
   - Objective check (to avoid pride bias): **HRV and resting heart rate.** If they deteriorate week after week, the body is already struggling.
3. **Desire and time are available.** An extra session **at the expense of sleep costs more than it brings.**

**Additional rule:** three "yes" *allows* moving up but **does not force it.**
- If the athlete is **still progressing** on the current plan → **change nothing.** Increasing now risks a progression that works, for no extra gain.
- **The right moment = all three signals green AND progress starting to plateau.**

## 8. Guidance for the AI coach

### 8.1 Information to gather from the athlete
- Current running frequency and typical session durations (to check the §5 prerequisite).
- Available days/time per week, and fixed constraints (work, family, sleep).
- Recent race times (5K/10K/half/marathon) and/or known HR values: **max HR, LT1 HR, LT2 HR**.
- Injury history and current niggles.
- Whether HRV / resting HR are tracked.
- Goal race and date, if any (to know how long general preparation can last).
- Preference for Variant A or B if at 6–7 sessions; access to hills.

### 8.2 Non-negotiable rules
1. **Pick one typical week and repeat it.** Change **one variable at a time.**
2. **Easy = conversational** (≈ ≤ 70–75% HRmax). Long run = same easy pace, no workout inside it.
3. **Threshold sessions stay between LT1 and LT2. Never above LT2.** Longer reps → slower pace.
4. Always use the progressive warm-up (or the athlete's proven equivalent).
5. Level 1: at least one rest day between sessions.
6. From level 3 on, second threshold session the day before the long run.
7. Variant B: **two easy days after session X.**
8. Move up only with **3 green signals + plateauing progress.**
9. When fatigue/pain appears: first remove/replace the most flexible element (level 5 Monday jog → session X → reduce threshold volume), rather than reshuffling the whole week.

### 8.3 Common mistakes to watch for
- Running all threshold reps at the same pace regardless of their length (usually too fast on long reps).
- Easy runs drifting into "moderate" pace.
- Adding progression or marathon-pace blocks to the long run.
- Increasing sessions while still progressing, or at the expense of sleep.
- Changing the plan every week (loses the "known cost / known benefit" property).

### 8.4 Quick reference
| Level | Sessions | Quality sessions | Threshold time / session | Key rule |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 3 | 1 threshold | ~20 min | Rest day between sessions |
| 2 | 4 | 2 threshold | ~20–25 min | 2 different formats |
| 3 | 5 | 2 threshold | ~20–30 min | 2nd threshold the day before long run |
| 4A | 6 | 3 threshold | ~20–30 min | Vary formats, always < LT2 |
| 4B | 6 | 2 threshold + X | ~20–30 min | 2 easy days after X (Sat X → Sun long → Mon rest) |
| 5A/5B | 7 | as level 4 | as level 4 | Monday jog = removable buffer |
